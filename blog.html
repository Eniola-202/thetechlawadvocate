Uber's Exit From Nigeria

The exit of a major digital platform from Nigeria raises a question that goes far beyond the platform itself: When a digital business exits a country, does its tax relationship with that country automatically end? The answer may not be as straightforward as it appears. Nigeria's tax framework has evolved significantly. The Nigeria Tax Act 2025, effective from 1 January 2026, reinforces the taxation of income derived from Nigeria by non-resident businesses and retains Significant Economic Presence (SEP) as an important basis for establishing a taxable nexus. This is particularly important for digital businesses. A platform may have no traditional brick-and-mortar operation comparable to a conventional business, yet it may have: • Nigerian customers and users; • substantial transactions connected with Nigeria; • Nigerian suppliers, drivers, merchants or service providers; • digital infrastructure serving the Nigerian market; • revenue generated from Nigerian economic activity; and • a sustained commercial relationship with the Nigerian market.

The central issue therefore becomes: Can a company simply switch off its Nigerian-facing platform and assume that all Nigerian tax questions switch off with it? That is a question worth asking in the context of Uber's exit however, the Uber situation should not be viewed merely as an Uber issue. It should be viewed as a warning signal for the wider digital economy. Foreign digital platforms operating in Nigeria need to ask difficult questions before they exit: Have we determined whether we have a Significant Economic Presence in Nigeria? Have we properly assessed our Nigerian-source income? Have our registration, filing, reporting and payment obligations been satisfied? Have we maintained adequate records to demonstrate our tax position?

And perhaps the most important question: Does leaving the Nigerian market extinguish liabilities that arose while the business was operating in Nigeria? The digital economy has changed the meaning of "presence." Therefore, a business does not necessarily need an office, factory or physical storefront to generate substantial economic value from a market. That is precisely why SEP matters. The Uber exit therefore provides an opportunity to revisit the tax architecture governing digital platforms in Nigeria and not to speculate about Uber's tax position, but to ask whether every digital platform operating in Nigeria has properly understood and complied with the rules that apply to it.
